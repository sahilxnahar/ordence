The business

A forty-person interiors business: one office and one warehouse, selling and installing fit-outs for offices and homes. Roughly sixty live projects at any time, each one a mix of bought-in materials and site labour, and a sales cycle measured in months rather than days.

Nothing about them is broken. They are profitable and growing, which is exactly the point at which the systems that got them here stop working.

Where the day went before

Not a horror story. Just the ordinary friction of a business that has outgrown its tools and has not noticed because it happened one workaround at a time.

  • Enquiries came from the website, IndiaMART, WhatsApp and walk-ins. Three people watched three of those channels. Nobody watched all four.
  • The pipeline was a shared spreadsheet. It was accurate on Monday morning and roughly accurate by Wednesday.
  • Quotes were built in Excel from a template, then re-typed into Tally when the job was won. Two of the twelve line items were wrong often enough to be a known joke.
  • Site material requirements were a WhatsApp message to the warehouse. Stock was a count somebody did on Saturdays.
  • Month-end took one senior person the better part of a week, reconciling what the spreadsheet said against what Tally said.

What changed, in the order it was done

One module at a time. This ordering is the recommendation, not an accident.

  1. First: capture and pipeline

    Every channel into one queue with an owner and an escalation. Four weeks in, the measurable change was not revenue — it was that no enquiry sat unanswered over a weekend, which had been happening about twice a month.

  2. Second: quotes from the catalogue

    Quotes built against real items at real prices, with GST computed rather than typed. The re-typing step into Tally disappeared, and so did the wrong line items.

  3. Third: stock and purchase

    Materials reserved against won projects, so what the warehouse saw was what was actually free. Purchase orders raised against real shortfalls instead of a guess.

  4. Fourth: the numbers

    Once everything ran on one ledger, margin per project stopped being a month-end reconstruction and became something you could look at on a Tuesday.

What was hard, and we are not going to pretend otherwise

The data migration took longer than anyone wanted, because eleven years of customer records had four spellings of the same company name and no consistent phone format. That work is unglamorous and it cannot be skipped — a clean system loaded with dirty data is just a more expensive spreadsheet.

Adoption was uneven for the first month. The people who lived in the spreadsheet were the slowest to move, which is normal: it was their spreadsheet and they were good at it. What changed it was not training. It was that the morning list was genuinely faster than opening four tabs.

And one module was scoped and then dropped — a production-scheduling piece that looked essential on paper and turned out to be a problem the warehouse team had already solved with a whiteboard. Building it would have cost money and been ignored.

Your business is not this one.
